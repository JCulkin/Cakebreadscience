Reminder: where charge comes from
An atom has the same number of protons (+) and electrons (−), so it has no overall charge.
Lose electrons → more + than − → positive ion.   Gain electrons → more − than + → negative ion.
charge = protons − electrons  ·  Metals (Groups 1, 2, 3) lose electrons; non-metals (Groups 5, 6, 7) gain them. Either way the ion ends up with a full outer shell, like a noble gas.  Shells hold 2, 8, 8.

How to write an ionic formula: the charges must cancel
Aluminium oxide: Al3+ and O2−. The lowest number both 3 and 2 go into is 6. 2 × Al3+ = 6+  and  3 × O2− = 6−  →  Al2O3 (overall charge 0).
If you need more than one of an ion made of several atoms, put it in brackets: Ca(OH)2, not CaOH2.

E  Work backwards the negative ions tell you the charge on the metal ion

Some metals, such as iron and copper, can form ions with different charges (Fe2+ or Fe3+). The formula tells you which one is present: the total positive charge must cancel the total negative charge, so add up the negative charges and share them between the metal ions.

F  Dot-and-cross diagrams: ionic bonding

Worked example: sodium chloride
Sodium's 1 outer electron (×) moves to chlorine. Na+ is left with a full shell underneath (not drawn); Cl− now has 8.
Checklist
  1. Show outer electrons only.
  2. Metal electrons as ×, non-metal as •.
  3. Metal ion: empty outer shell, so just write the symbol.
  4. Non-metal ion: a full shell of 8.
  5. Square brackets round each ion, charge outside, top right.
  6. Charges must cancel: you may need 2 or 3 of one ion. Draw every ion.

Transverse & Longitudinal Waves

A wave is a way of moving energy (and information) from one place to another. The particles wobble back and forth around a fixed point. They do not travel along with the wave.

IGCSE Edexcel 3.2 – 3.9, 3.14, 3.23
The only difference between the two types is the direction the particles oscillate compared with the direction the energy travels.

Applet 1Transverse waves

The particles move up and down, at 90° (perpendicular) to the direction the energy travels. Watch the red particle: it never moves sideways.

Watch one particle: displacement–time graph

This graph shows the red particle's position over time. The time for one full wave is the period, T. Frequency is how many waves pass each second: f = 1 ÷ T.

Applet 2Longitudinal waves

The particles move backwards and forwards, parallel to the direction the energy travels. This makes regions where the particles are squashed together (compressions) and spread apart (rarefactions).

C = compression (particles squashed together)  ·  R = rarefaction (particles spread apart). The dashed red line is the red particles' rest position.

Exam trap: a graph of a longitudinal wave looks like a transverse wave. It plots how far each particle is pushed left or right (up = right, down = left) against position. The particles themselves still only move left and right.

SummarySpot the difference

TransverseLongitudinal
Oscillations are…Perpendicular (at 90°) to the direction of energy transferParallel to the direction of energy transfer
FeaturesCrests (peaks) and troughsCompressions and rarefactions
Wavelength measuredCrest to next crest (or any point to the next identical point)Compression to next compression
ExamplesAll electromagnetic waves (light, radio, X-rays…), ripples on water, a rope shaken up and down, seismic S-wavesSound, ultrasound, a slinky pushed and pulled, seismic P-waves
Travels through a vacuum?Electromagnetic waves canNo. Sound needs particles (a medium) to pass the vibration along

Key words (3.3)Describing any wave

AmplitudeThe maximum displacement of a point from its rest position. Unit: m
Wavelength, λThe distance from one point on a wave to the same point on the next wave. Unit: m
Frequency, fThe number of waves passing a point each second. Unit: hertz (Hz)
Period, TThe time taken for one complete wave to pass a point. Unit: s · f = 1 ÷ T
WavefrontA line joining points on a wave that are all at the same stage (for example, all the crests). See Applet 3
Wave speed, vHow fast the energy moves. v = f × λ · Unit: m/s

Applet 3Wavefronts and the Doppler effect

Seen from above, each circle is a wavefront (one crest). Make the source move: in front of it the wavefronts bunch up (shorter wavelength, higher frequency); behind it they spread out (longer wavelength, lower frequency). This is the Doppler effect. It's why an ambulance siren sounds higher as it comes towards you and lower as it drives away.

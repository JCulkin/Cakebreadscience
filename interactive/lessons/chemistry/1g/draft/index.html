What is a covalent bond? 1.441.45

Non-metal atoms need to gain electrons to complete their outer shell. When two non-metal atoms meet, neither can pull electrons completely away from the other — so they share pairs of electrons instead.

Key idea

A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons between two atoms. The bond is the strong electrostatic attraction between the negatively charged shared pair of electrons and the positively charged nuclei of both atoms.

Both positive nuclei pull on the same negative shared pair — holding the atoms tightly together.

Ionic vs covalent at a glance

Metal + non-metalIonic: electrons are transferred, forming oppositely charged ions
Non-metal + non-metalCovalent: pairs of electrons are shared

Single bond = 1 shared pair (2 e⁻) · Double bond = 2 shared pairs (4 e⁻) · Triple bond = 3 shared pairs (6 e⁻).

Examiner tip

When a 2-mark question asks "Describe a covalent bond", never stop at "sharing electrons". Write both halves: a shared pair of electrons (1) and the electrostatic attraction between the shared electrons and both positive nuclei (1).

Dot-and-cross diagrams 1.46

We draw covalent bonding using outer-shell electrons only. Overlap the outer circles where two atoms bond, and put every shared pair inside the overlap. Use crosses (×) for one atom's electrons and dots (●) for the other's.

Worked example — Water (H₂O)
  1. Count outer electrons: Oxygen is in Group 6 (6 outer e⁻, needs 2 more to reach 8). Each Hydrogen has 1 outer e⁻ (needs 1 more to reach 2).
  2. Pair them up: Oxygen shares 1 electron with each of the two H atoms → 2 single bonds (2 shared pairs in the overlaps).
  3. Add lone pairs: Oxygen used 2 of its 6 outer electrons in bonds, leaving 4 unshared electrons = 2 lone pairs on its outer ring.
  4. Final count check: Each H has 2 electrons in its circle; O has 8 electrons in its circle. ✓
Now you try: how many shared pairs and lone pairs are in carbon dioxide (CO₂)?
Carbon (Group 4) needs 4 more electrons → forms two double bonds (one to each oxygen) = 4 shared pairs in total (2 pairs in each overlap). Carbon has 0 lone pairs. Each oxygen (Group 6) uses 2 electrons in its double bond, leaving 2 lone pairs on each oxygen (4 lone pairs in total). Check it in the explorer below!

Common mistakes to avoid
  • Forgetting lone pairs on O, N, F, Cl or Br — always check that each non-H atom has 8 outer electrons in total.
  • Drawing a double bond with only 2 electrons — a double bond is 2 shared pairs = 4 electrons in the overlap!
  • Giving hydrogen 8 electrons — hydrogen's first shell is full with just 2 electrons.

Simple molecular substances 1.471.481.51

Most covalent substances — such as water (H2O), carbon dioxide (CO2), methane (CH4), oxygen (O2) and chlorine (Cl2) — are made of small, separate molecules.

Key idea — Low melting & boiling points

Inside each molecule the covalent bonds are strong. Between neighbouring molecules there are only weak intermolecular forces of attraction. Melting or boiling only overcomes these weak intermolecular forces (never the covalent bonds). That takes little energy, so simple molecular substances are gases, liquids, or low-melting solids.

Inside each H₂O

Strong covalent bonds hold H and O together. They do not break when water boils.

Between H₂O molecules

Weak intermolecular forces pull molecules together. Boiling only overcomes these.

Examiner warning — #1 lost mark in this topic

NEVER write "covalent bonds are weak" or "covalent bonds break when methane boils". Examiners immediately award 0 marks for that sentence. Always say: "weak intermolecular forces between molecules take little energy to overcome."

Double Award only · 1.48

Bigger molecules → higher boiling point

As the relative molecular mass (Mr) increases, the intermolecular forces between molecules get stronger, so more energy is needed to overcome them and the boiling point rises.

Do covalent substances conduct electricity? 1.51

Covalent substances do not usually conduct electricity — whether solid, liquid or dissolved — because molecules have no overall charge, and there are no free-moving electrons or mobile ions to carry a current. (Graphite is the famous exception — see Step 5!)

Giant covalent structures 1.49

A few non-metals — especially carbon and silicon — do not stop at small separate molecules. Instead, millions of atoms join together into one huge continuous giant covalent lattice.

Key idea — Very high melting points

To melt a giant covalent structure (such as diamond, graphite or silicon dioxide, SiO2), you must break many strong covalent bonds throughout the lattice. That requires a large amount of energy, so they are solids with very high melting and boiling points.

Common mistake

Don't confuse giant covalent with giant ionic! Both have high melting points, but giant covalent lattices are made of neutral atoms sharing electrons — there are no ions, so molten diamond or sand will not conduct electricity.

Diamond, graphite & fullerenes 1.50 Double Award only

Diamond, graphite and C60 fullerene are all made of pure carbon (different structural forms of the same element are called allotropes). Their properties are totally different because their atoms are bonded in different arrangements.

PropertyDiamondGraphiteC60 fullerene
StructureGiant covalent (3D lattice)Giant covalent (hexagonal layers)Simple molecular (hollow balls)
Covalent bonds per C433
HardnessExtremely hard: every atom locked in by 4 strong bonds in all directionsSoft & slippery: weak forces between layers allow layers to slide over each otherSoft: weak intermolecular forces between separate C60 balls

Examiner tip — How to score full marks on graphite
  • Why does graphite conduct? Each carbon forms 3 covalent bonds → leaves 1 delocalised electron per carbon that is free to move through the structure and carry charge.
  • Why is graphite soft / used in pencils? Carbon atoms are in layers with only weak forces between layers, so the layers can slide over each other easily.
  • Why doesn't C60 conduct? Even though each C forms 3 bonds, C60 consists of separate molecules so electrons cannot move freely from one molecule to the next.

  1. 0
  2. 1

  4. 3

When water boils, what is broken?

  1. Covalent bonds between O and H
  2. Intermolecular forces between water molecules
  3. Ionic bonds
  4. Both covalent bonds and intermolecular forces

Which has the highest boiling point?

  1. Methane, CH4
  2. Ethane, C2H6
  3. Propane, C3H8
  4. Butane, C4H10

Why does diamond not conduct electricity?

  1. It has no electrons
  2. All four outer electrons of each carbon are used in covalent bonds
  3. Its ions cannot move
  4. It is a simple molecular substance

Explain why methane has a low boiling point.

  • Methane is made of simple molecules / has weak intermolecular forces between molecules
  • Little energy is needed to overcome these forces (covalent bonds are not broken)

Explain why graphite conducts electricity and is soft, but diamond is hard and does not conduct.

  • Graphite: each carbon is bonded to 3 others
  • One electron per carbon is delocalised and can move through the structure to carry charge
  • Graphite has layers held by weak forces, so the layers slide over each other
  • Diamond: each carbon is bonded to 4 others in a rigid 3D network of strong covalent bonds
  • Diamond has no delocalised electrons

Draw a dot-and-cross diagram for carbon dioxide, CO2. Show outer electrons only.

  • Two shared pairs (a double bond) between C and each O
  • Two lone pairs on each O; C with no lone pairs (8 electrons around each atom)

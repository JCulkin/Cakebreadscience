What is a covalent bond? 1.441.45

Non-metal atoms need to gain electrons to fill their outer shell. When two non-metals meet, neither can take electrons from the other, so they share them instead.

Covalent bond: a shared pair of electrons between two atoms. The bond is the electrostatic attraction between the shared pair (negative) and the nuclei of both atoms (positive).

Both nuclei are pulled towards the same shared pair, and that holds the atoms together.

Which bond forms?

Metal + non-metalIonic: electrons are transferred
Non-metal + non-metalCovalent: electrons are shared

Single bond = 1 shared pair · double = 2 pairs · triple = 3 pairs.

Dot-and-cross diagrams 1.46

Draw each atom's outer shell as a circle. Overlap the circles where atoms bond, and put the shared pairs in the overlap. Use crosses for one atom's electrons and dots for the other's. Every atom should end with a full outer shell: 8 electrons, or 2 for hydrogen.

The spec expects all of these: diatomic molecules (H2, O2, N2, Cl2, HCl); H2O, NH3, CO2; and CH4, C2H6, C2H4 and CH3Cl. Only the outer shells are shown.

Common mistakes: forgetting the lone pairs on O, N and Cl; drawing a double bond as just two electrons; putting shared electrons outside the overlap; giving hydrogen more than 2 electrons.

Simple molecular substances 1.471.48

Most covalent substances are made of small, separate molecules, for example water, carbon dioxide, methane and chlorine.

Inside each molecule the covalent bonds are strong. Between molecules there are only weak intermolecular forces. Melting or boiling only breaks the intermolecular forces, not the covalent bonds. That takes little energy, so these substances are gases or liquids, or solids with low melting and boiling points.

Solid lines: strong covalent bonds (not broken on boiling). Dashed lines: weak intermolecular forces (broken on boiling).

Bigger molecules → stronger intermolecular forces → higher boiling point

Covalent substances do not usually conduct electricity 1.51. The molecules have no overall charge, and there are no free electrons or ions to carry a current.

Giant covalent structures 1.49

Some non-metals form a giant lattice: millions of atoms all joined by covalent bonds, with no separate molecules. Examples are diamond, graphite and silicon dioxide (sand).

To melt a giant covalent structure you must break many strong covalent bonds. That takes a lot of energy, so they are solids with very high melting and boiling points. They are insoluble in water, and they don't conduct electricity (except graphite).

What is a covalent bond?

  1. The attraction between oppositely charged ions
  2. A shared pair of electrons between two atoms
  3. A sea of delocalised electrons
  4. A weak force between molecules

How many shared pairs of electrons are in a nitrogen molecule, N2?

  1. 1
  2. 2
  3. 3
  4. 6

Which molecule contains a double covalent bond?

  1. CH4
  2. NH3
  3. HCl
  4. CO2

How many lone pairs (non-bonding pairs) are on the oxygen atom in water?

  1. 0
  2. 1
  3. 2
  4. 3

When water boils, what is broken?

  1. Covalent bonds between O and H
  2. Intermolecular forces between water molecules
  3. Ionic bonds
  4. Both covalent bonds and intermolecular forces

Which has the highest boiling point?

  1. Methane, CH4
  2. Ethane, C2H6
  3. Propane, C3H8
  4. Butane, C4H10

Why does diamond not conduct electricity?

  1. It has no electrons
  2. All four outer electrons of each carbon are used in covalent bonds
  3. Its ions cannot move
  4. It is a simple molecular substance

Explain why methane has a low boiling point.

  • Methane is made of simple molecules / has weak intermolecular forces
  • Little energy is needed to overcome them (covalent bonds are not broken)

Explain why graphite conducts electricity and is soft, but diamond is hard and does not conduct.

  • Graphite: each carbon bonded to 3 others
  • so one electron per carbon is delocalised and can move through the structure / carry charge
  • Graphite has layers held by weak forces, so the layers slide over each other
  • Diamond: each carbon bonded to 4 others in a rigid 3D network of strong covalent bonds
  • Diamond has no delocalised electrons

Draw a dot-and-cross diagram for carbon dioxide, CO2. Show outer electrons only.

  • Two shared pairs (a double bond) between C and each O
  • Two lone pairs on each O; C with no lone pairs (8 electrons around each atom)

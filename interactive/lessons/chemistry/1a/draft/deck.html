Chemistry 1a.1

States of matter

What is matter?

Anything that has mass and volume
Mass: the amount of matter (kg)
Volume: the space it takes up (m3 or L)
Solid
Liquid
Gas

Think

You pump air into a football.
Does its mass increase, decrease or stay the same?
→ It increases: air is matter, so it has mass.

The particle model

Solid
Liquid
Gas
All matter is made of tiny particles that are constantly moving or vibrating

Solids

  • Fixed positions in a regular lattice
  • Vibrate about fixed positions (more when heated)
  • Fixed shape and fixed volume
  • High density — cannot be compressed
Forces of attraction: Strong

Liquids

  • Close together (touching), random arrangement
  • Move past each other (faster when heated)
  • Fixed volume, takes the shape of the container
  • Cannot be compressed easily
Forces of attraction: Weak

Gases

  • Random and far apart
  • Move freely in all directions (faster when heated)
  • No fixed shape or volume — fill any container
  • Low density — easily compressed
Forces of attraction: Very weak

Chemistry 1a.2

Changes of state

Changes of state

Caused by changes in temperature and/or pressure
  • Change the particles' kinetic energy
  • They are reversible physical changes
  • Also called interconversions or transitions
Dry ice (solid CO₂) sublimating into gas

Changes of state

Try it: heat and cool

Melting and freezing

  • Heating a solid: particles vibrate more
  • At the melting point they overcome fixed positions and move around: melting
  • Cooling a liquid: at the freezing point forces hold them in place again
melting point = freezing point

Boiling and condensing

  • Heating a liquid: particles move faster
  • At the boiling point they overcome all forces of attraction
  • Particles spread far apart → boiling
Condensing: gas → liquid when cooled

Evaporation

Liquid → gas without boiling
  • Fastest particles at the surface escape into the air
  • Happens at any temperature below the boiling point
  • Faster at higher temperature, wind, or wider surface
Boiling forms gas bubbles throughout the whole liquid at the boiling point.

Sublimation and deposition

Sublimation: solid → gas
  • e.g. dry ice (solid CO2)
Deposition: gas → solid
  • e.g. frost forming in a freezer

Temperature and state

Water melts at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C
What state is water at 24 °C?
→ Liquid (24 °C is between 0 °C and 100 °C)
  • At exactly 0 °C or 100 °C, it can be in either state
  • Compare the given temperature to the melting and boiling points

Solid, liquid or gas at 25 °C?

Which has stronger forces?

Air or olive oil?
  • Air is a gas at room temperature (boils at about −195 °C)
  • Olive oil is a liquid at room temperature (boiling point well above 25 °C)
Stronger forces → more energy needed to separate particles → higher boiling point

Chemistry 1a.3

Diffusion

Diffusion

Particles spread from high to low concentration
  • Caused by their random motion
  • Keep moving and colliding until evenly spread out

Simulation: diffusion

Particles are fast!

Gas particles travel at hundreds of m/s (hydrogen ≈ 1700 m/s)
So why is diffusion slow?
  • Particles constantly collide and change direction
  • Higher temperature → faster particles → faster diffusion

Salt or flour?

Key words (1 of 2)

Solution: the mixture formed when a substance dissolves
Solute: the substance that dissolves (e.g. salt)
Solvent: the liquid it dissolves in (usually water)

Key words (2 of 2)

Dissolve: a soluble substance mixing completely into a liquid
Soluble / insoluble: can / cannot dissolve
Saturated: no more solute can dissolve

Where does the solute go?

Same number of particles, same size, just spread out
Flour stays grouped together → insoluble, still visible

Is sugar water pure?

No! Pure = only one substance. A solution is a mixture (separable by physical processes).

Saturated

No more solute can dissolve
  • Different solvents have different saturation points
  • At 20 °C, 1 litre of water dissolves about 357 g of salt
  • Extra solid settles at the bottom as undissolved crystals

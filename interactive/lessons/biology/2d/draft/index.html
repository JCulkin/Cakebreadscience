Three ways in and out of cells 2.15

Cells take in substances they need (oxygen, glucose, amino acids, water) and remove waste (carbon dioxide, urea) by three processes: diffusion, osmosis and active transport.

Key idea — Diffusion

Diffusion: the net movement of particles from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration (down a concentration gradient). It is passive — no energy from respiration is needed.

Four factors that speed up diffusion 2.16

FactorFaster diffusion when…Why?
TemperatureHigher temperatureParticles have more kinetic energy and move faster
DistanceShorter distanceParticles have less far to travel (e.g. 1-cell-thick alveolus wall)
Concentration gradientSteeper (bigger difference)Greater net movement from high to low concentration
Surface area : volumeLarger SA : V ratioMore area available for particles to enter relative to volume (e.g. alveoli, villi, root hairs)

Worked example — Surface area to volume ratio (SA : V)
  1. 1 cm cube: Surface area = 6 × (1 × 1) = 6 cm². Volume = 1 × 1 × 1 = 1 cm³ → SA : V = 6 : 1.
  2. 2 cm cube: Surface area = 6 × (2 × 2) = 24 cm². Volume = 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 cm³ → SA : V = 24 : 8 = 3 : 1.
Now you try: calculate the SA : V ratio of a 3 cm cube.
Surface area = 6 × (3 × 3) = 54 cm². Volume = 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 cm³. Ratio = 54 : 27 = 2 : 1 — as organisms get bigger, SA : V gets smaller!

Active transport 2.15

Key idea

Active transport: movement of particles from low concentration to high concentration (against a concentration gradient), using energy released by respiration.

  • Plant roots: root hair cells absorb mineral ions from dilute soil water into a more concentrated root cell.
  • Small intestine: absorbs glucose into the blood even when blood concentration is higher than in the gut.
  • Cells doing lots of active transport contain many mitochondria to release energy by aerobic respiration.
Examiner warning — Active transport vs diffusion

Active transport moves substances against a concentration gradient (low → high) and requires energy from respiration. If respiration stops (e.g. no oxygen in waterlogged soil), active transport stops!

Osmosis 2.15

Key idea

Osmosis: the net movement of water from a dilute solution (high water potential) to a more concentrated solution (low water potential) across a partially permeable membrane.

  • A partially permeable membrane has tiny pores: small water molecules pass through, while larger sugar or protein molecules cannot.
  • Visking tubing practical 2.17: tubing filled with concentrated (0.5%) sugar solution in a dilute (0.1%) sugar beaker gains water by osmosis and swells.

Plant cells and water

In pure water, water enters a plant cell by osmosis and it becomes turgid (firm — the strong cellulose cell wall stops it bursting). In a concentrated salt or sugar solution, water leaves the cell: it becomes flaccid, then plasmolysed as the membrane pulls away from the cell wall.

Examiner warning — Osmosis is WATER ONLY

Never say "sugar moves by osmosis"! Osmosis is always the net movement of water molecules from a dilute solution (high water potential) to a more concentrated solution (low water potential) across a partially permeable membrane.

Practical: osmosis in potato 2.17 practical

  1. Cut 5 identical potato cylinders with a cork borer, remove skin, and trim to the same length (4 cm).
  2. Blot dry with paper towel (to remove surface water) and record each starting mass.
  3. Place each cylinder in a different salt or sucrose concentration (0.0 to 1.0 M) for the same time (e.g. 15–30 minutes).
  4. Remove, blot dry gently, and record each final mass.
  5. Calculate % change in mass = (change in mass ÷ starting mass) × 100 so cylinders with slightly different starting masses can be compared fairly.

B  Practical: investigating motion

IGCSE Edexcel Physics · Topic booklet
1.4, 1.5 · investigate the motion of everyday objects such as toy cars or tennis balls
Name:
Key ideas
  • To find a speed, measure a distance (metre rule or tape measure) and a time (stopwatch or light gates), then use speed = distance ÷ time.
  • Light gates are better than a stopwatch: they remove the human reaction time, so the times are more accurate.
  • Repeat each measurement, spot and ignore anomalous results, then calculate a mean.
  • Independent variable = what you change. Dependent = what you measure. Control = what you keep the same.

C  Acceleration

IGCSE Edexcel Physics · Topic booklet
1.6 · acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time taken
Name:
Key ideas
  • acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time taken   a = (v − u) ÷ t
  • u = initial velocity, v = final velocity (m/s); t = time (s); a in m/s².
  • An acceleration of 3 m/s² means the velocity goes up by 3 m/s every second.
  • A negative acceleration is a deceleration (slowing down).
  • Velocity is speed in a given direction, so changing direction is also an acceleration.
Rearranging
v = u + a × t
u = v − a × t
t = (v − u) ÷ a

Converting
km/h → m/s: ÷ 3.6
1 km = 1000 m

D  Velocity–time graphs

IGCSE Edexcel Physics · Topic booklet
1.7–1.9 · velocity–time graphs: gradient = acceleration, area = distance
Name:
Key ideas
  • The gradient of a velocity–time graph = acceleration.
  • A horizontal line = constant velocity (acceleration is zero).
  • A line sloping down = deceleration.
  • The area between the line and the time axis = distance travelled. Split it into rectangles (base × height) and triangles (½ × base × height).
  • Don’t confuse this with a distance–time graph: here a flat line does not mean stopped.

F  Forces, vectors and scalars

IGCSE Edexcel Physics · Topic booklet
1.11–1.14 · effects of forces, types of force, vectors and scalars, force is a vector
Name:
Key ideas
  • A force can change an object’s speed, its direction of motion, or its shape.
  • Forces act between two objects. Measured in newtons (N).
  • Non-contact forces act at a distance: gravitational (weight), electrostatic, magnetic.
  • Contact forces: friction, air resistance (drag), tension, normal reaction, upthrust.
  • A scalar has size (magnitude) only, e.g. speed, distance, mass, time, energy.
  • A vector has size and direction, e.g. velocity, displacement, force, weight, acceleration.
  • Force is a vector, so we draw it as an arrow: length = size, arrowhead = direction.

G  Resultant forces and friction

IGCSE Edexcel Physics · Topic booklet
1.15–1.16 · resultant force along a line; friction opposes motion
Name:
Key ideas
  • The resultant force is the single force that has the same effect as all the forces acting together.
  • Forces in the same direction add; forces in opposite directions subtract. Give the size and the direction.
  • Resultant = 0 → forces are balanced: a stationary object stays still; a moving object keeps the same velocity.
  • Resultant ≠ 0 → the object accelerates in the direction of the resultant (speeds up, slows down or changes direction).
  • Friction always acts in the opposite direction to the motion. Air resistance (drag) is friction from the air and gets bigger as speed increases.

H  Force, mass, acceleration and weight

IGCSE Edexcel Physics · Topic booklet
1.17–1.18 · F = m × a; W = m × g
Name:
Key ideas
  • force = mass × acceleration   F = m × a
  • F is the resultant (unbalanced) force in N, m in kg, a in m/s².
  • Bigger force → bigger acceleration. Bigger mass → smaller acceleration for the same force.
  • weight = mass × gravitational field strength   W = m × g
  • Mass (kg) is the amount of matter: the same everywhere. Weight (N) is the force of gravity on it: it depends on g.
  • On Earth g = 10 N/kg.

I  Stopping distances

IGCSE Edexcel Physics · Topic booklet
1.19–1.20 · stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance; factors that affect it
Name:
Key ideas
  • stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance
  • Thinking distance: distance travelled during the driver’s reaction time (before the brakes are on).
  • It increases with speed and with a longer reaction time: tiredness, alcohol, drugs, distractions (e.g. a phone).
  • Braking distance: distance travelled while the brakes are on.
  • It increases with speed, greater mass, poor road conditions (wet, icy, loose gravel) and worn tyres or brakes.
  • Doubling the speed doubles the thinking distance but makes the braking distance about four times bigger.

J  Falling objects and terminal velocity

IGCSE Edexcel Physics · Topic booklet
1.21 · forces on falling objects; why they reach a terminal velocity
Name:
Key ideas
  • A falling object has two forces on it: weight (down, stays the same) and air resistance (up, increases as speed increases).
  • At first only weight acts, so it accelerates at 10 m/s². As it speeds up, air resistance grows, the resultant force gets smaller, so the acceleration decreases.
  • When air resistance = weight, the resultant force is zero, so the velocity stays constant: this is terminal velocity.
  • Opening a parachute increases the surface area, so air resistance becomes bigger than weight: the resultant is upwards and the skydiver slows down until the forces balance again at a lower terminal velocity.

K  Springs, Hooke’s law and elastic behaviour

IGCSE Edexcel Physics · Topic booklet
1.22–1.24 · extension against force for springs, wires and rubber bands; Hooke’s law; elastic behaviour
Name:
Key ideas
  • extension = stretched length − original length
  • Hooke’s law: extension is directly proportional to force. The graph is a straight line through the origin.
  • This is only true for the initial linear region, up to the limit of proportionality. After that the line curves.
  • Elastic behaviour: the material returns to its original shape when the force is removed.
  • Stretched too far, it is permanently deformed (stays longer).
  • Springs and metal wires obey Hooke’s law at first; rubber bands do not (their graph is curved).

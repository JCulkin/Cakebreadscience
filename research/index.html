How the site fits together one job per layer — no duplication

Edexcel International GCSE Science (Double & Single Award) assesses roughly 40% AO1 knowledge, 40% AO2 application and calculations, and 20% AO3 practical and data skills. Rather than cramming everything into one tool, each part of Cakebread Science owns a distinct stage of learning.

1. Secondary Science Pedagogy & Classroom Practice

EEF: Improving Secondary Science

The Education Endowment Foundation identifies seven pillars for high-impact secondary science: tackling preconceptions, developing self-regulation, critiquing models, strengthening memory, making practical work purposeful, explicitly teaching the language of science, and giving structured feedback.

On this site: Misconception refutation boxes in study guides/worksheets, a single Examiner Key Words database across five tools, and practical/AO3 questions built into every spec point.
EEF Secondary Science ↗

CogSciSci, Rosenshine & SLOP

Barak Rosenshine (popularised by Tom Sherrington) and the UK science teacher collective CogSciSci (Adam Boxer et al.) translate cognitive architecture into lesson sequences: daily review, small steps, worked models, high initial success (~80%), and SLOP (Shed Loads of Practice) to build automaticity before complex exam problems.

On this site: Stepped projector decks, Topic Booklets ramping from guided recall to unguided synthesis, and generator-backed calculation drills.

2. Classroom Craft: Mr Barton & TeacherToolkit

Craig Barton (Mr Barton): Diagnostics, Worked Examples & Variation

Craig Barton's work across Diagnostic Questions, Variation Theory, and Tips for Teachers shapes how questions and practice are written here:

  • Diagnostic distractors: Every wrong MCQ choice targets a specific, common misconception so wrong answers immediately reveal why a student got stuck.
  • Example–Problem Pairs: A fully worked model placed directly beside a parallel "Now you try" problem cuts extraneous working-memory load.
  • Variation Theory: Changing one feature at a time across consecutive questions forces learners to notice the underlying rule (Reflect → Expect → Check → Explain).
Mr Barton Maths ↗ Variation Theory ↗ Tips for Teachers ↗

Ross Morrison McGill (TeacherToolkit): Feedback, DIRT & Workload

TeacherToolkit bridges cognitive science with sustainable teaching — replacing unsustainable written marking with low-stakes retrieval, whole-class feedback, and DIRT (Directed Improvement and Reflection Time) where students actively correct and redraft their work.

Research highlighted on TeacherToolkit also stresses a crucial reality: students often dislike retrieval practice because it feels effortful compared to re-reading, so we explicitly explain why productive struggle works.

On this site: Attempt-first black-then-purple mark schemes, automated Admin homework/spec tracking, and a plain-English student guide.

3. Memory, Spacing & Metacognition

Retrieval, Spacing & Desirable Difficulties

Roediger & Karpicke (the Testing Effect) and Cepeda et al. show that recalling from memory across spaced intervals outperforms re-reading and cramming. Robert & Elizabeth Bjork explain why: letting retrieval strength dip slightly between days turns recall into a desirable difficulty that dramatically raises long-term storage strength.

On this site: Spec points require right answers on two different days, decay to "rusty" after 21 days, exclude same-day Second Wind retries from securing, and homework defaults to 1 short mission on 2 different days.
The Learning Scientists ↗ RetrievalPractice.org ↗

Metacognition (+7 months) & Homework (+5 months)

The EEF rates Metacognition & Self-Regulated Learning as one of the highest-impact, lowest-cost levers (+7 months), and purposeful secondary Homework at +5 months — stressing quality, curriculum link, and independent achievability over sheer volume.

Because students routinely overestimate what they know (the Illusion of Competence), Spec Trackers explicitly compare self-rated confidence against 2-day quiz proof and flag blind spots with Check these.

4. Beautiful, Cognitively Clear Websites & Printed Material

Web Clarity: Cognitive Load, Scannability & Accessibility

Following Nielsen Norman Group, GOV.UK Design System, and WCAG 2.2, every student screen aims to cut extraneous load so working memory stays on the science:

  • One clear primary action: Student Home puts teacher work and a single ⚡ Start now button first, tucking secondary lists behind collapsible drawers.
  • Calm, glare-free reading: Cream paper (#eeeae0) with dark ink (#111), 16–17px sans-serif type, 1.45–1.6 line height, left-aligned text, and bolding instead of long italics or ALL CAPS.
  • Never colour alone: Status badges always pair colour with clear text labels and consistent subject coding.
NN/g Cognitive Load ↗ GOV.UK Design System ↗

Print & Visual Clarity: Caviglioli, Mayer & BDA

Grounded in Oliver Caviglioli (Dual Coding with Teachers), Richard Mayer (Coherence & Spatial Contiguity), and the British Dyslexia Association style guide:

  • Zero seductive details: Diagrams exist to teach (processes, comparisons, apparatus) — never decorative clipart or faint watermarks behind questions.
  • Integrated labels: Labels sit directly on diagrams so eyes don't split attention between a picture and a separate legend.
  • Photocopy-first greyscale: Dedicated monochrome SVG emblems, stroke widths ≥ 0.35 mm, dashed/labelled graph lines, and light tints instead of solid dark toner blocks.
Oliver Caviglioli ↗ British Dyslexia Association ↗

5. Game-Based Learning & Motivation Questing & Breadventure Land

Self-Determination Theory & Sailer & Homner (2020)

In their landmark 2020 meta-analysis in Educational Psychology Review, Michael Sailer & Lisa Homner found meaningful positive effects of gamification on cognitive (g = 0.49), motivational (g = 0.36), and behavioural (g = 0.25) outcomes — with the biggest gains coming from game fiction (narrative, world, avatars) and combining collaboration with light competition, rather than bare points and badges.

Grounded in Ryan & Deci's Self-Determination Theory, Cakebread Questing supports:

  • Autonomy: Students choose topics, gear, or plain non-game Practice mode with identical progress.
  • Competence: Character power grows directly from genuine 2-day secured science points, not aimless grinding.
  • Relatedness: Everyone explores Breadventure Land together and teams up on midnight bosses.

Avoiding Gamification Pitfalls

Game-based learning research (Plass, Homer & Kinzer; Deci's overjustification effect) warns that poorly designed game loops encourage rapid guessing and demotivate lower-attaining students. Cakebread Questing deliberately guards against this:

  • No spam-clicking rewards: Two-different-days securing and short 10-question missions stop "keep clicking until 20 right" frustration.
  • Graceful failure: Missed questions give immediate elaborative feedback and a Second Wind chance to learn without punishing progress.
  • Healthy leaderboards: Rolling 7-day boards reward recent consistency rather than cementing an untouchable top three all year.

6. Working with Families at Home high impact, zero IGCSE science required

Research by Alma Harris & Janet Goodall and the EEF Guidance Report on Working with Parents reveals a clear finding for secondary school: 20–22% of secondary parents feel unable to help with GCSE / IGCSE subject content — yet parents do not need to act as science tutors to make a major difference. What matters most is calm routine, showing interest in learning, and supporting independence rather than policing homework.

How families can help in 5 minutes a week (no science knowledge needed)

1. Protect a short, phone-free slot

A 10-minute session on two different days beats a stressful Sunday-night cram. Ask "Which two days this week will you do your science missions?" rather than hovering over every click.

2. Quiz from Flashcards or Key Words

Open the student's Flashcards or Upcoming Test Study Guide. Read the front aloud and check if they include the bold key words on the back — you don't need to know the topic yourself.

3. Praise consistency, not just scores

Struggling to recall something from last week means the brain is building stronger memory. Celebrate turning up for Day 2 and fixing mistakes rather than chasing 100% first time.
